Lewis and Clark, 200 Years Later

by Ignacio Somoza
Beat 1: The Road Trip

In March of 2026, I flew to San Francisco with five friends, intending to drive up to Seattle over five days.

We hit many of the major parks of the Pacific Northwest.

Mt. Hood,

Mt. St. Helens,

Mt. Rainier (from afar),

Cape Disappointment (disappointing) and Cape Flattery (flattering),

and my personal favorite - Mount Olympus.

Wait, that's not Olympus. We didn't actually get to see it.
We didn't actually get to see Olympus. While I was dying to see the mountain both for its name and its legacy, I wasn't aware that the mountain typically is not visible from outside the national park. I left feeling my trip was incomplete, vowing to return another day to see the mountain.

Beat 2: The Map in the Basement

One month later, in the basement of Beinecke, a map caught my attention - an intricately drawn, extremely detailed, aesthetically pleasing map of western North America.

After staring at it for a long time (including with a magnifying glass), I was told by the library's curator, Julia Hernández, that this map was drawn by William Clark himself in 1810 after Lewis and Clark's journey westward.

Beat 3: What Else?

After noticing Olympus, I started looking for other omissions.

Step 1 — Mount Adams: unmarked, only 35 miles from St. Helens.

This was fascinating, considering Adams’ prominence can be seen from many places along the Columbia River Gorge (including, vaguely, by my friends and I)

which was the path Lewis and Clark traversed around late October and early November of 1805 - around the same time, Clark writes in his journal about extreme rain, hail, and thunderstorms [add journal entry]. I don't blame him for not seeing the mountain.


"a tremendious thunder Storm abt. 3 oClock this morning accompanied by wind from the S W. and Hail, this Storm of hard Clap's thunder Lighting and hail until about 6 oClock at intervals it then became light for a Short time when the heavens became darkined by a black Cloud from the S, W, & a hard rain Suckceeded which lasted untill 12 oClock with a hard wind which raised the Seas tremendiously high braking with great force and fury against the rocks & trees on which we lie, as our Situation became Seriously dangerous,"

Continuing on mountains, the North Cascade's magnificence is understated and even completely ignored.

Today, Cascades National Park is one of the Pacific Northwest's most beautiful sites and sights, and yet remains the 2nd least visited national park in the contiguous United States. To me, this suggests that not too much has changed since Lewis and Clark made their journey west - many still end up missing these mountains.

Apart from the Rockies, which are well-detailed, large mountain ranges are underrepresented on the map. In addition to the Cascades, California's Sierra Nevada and extremely mountainous landscape (with the tallest peaks in the continental US!) is depicted by a blank slate, and a long row of mini mountains on the coast.

Crater Lake, one of America's most famous lakes and national parks, is also not on the map. In fact, it wasn't discovered by settlers until 1853. The lake, famous for its deep blue waters, island in the center, and being the deepest lake in the United States, is also inaccessible for most of the year.

The upper part of the map is much more detailed than the rest (and also in a different language).

So, much of the map is left blank, or described in miniscule detail. Lewis and Clark left from St. Louis in 1804 with little to no idea of what the Western half of the continent looked like. Their task was to map it. They didn't know that they were missing many of these landmarks.

Even if Clark had been omniscient and been able to map the entire continent accurate to the millimeter, it would look very different today.

Beat 5: The Blank Map

Today, more than 200 years after this map was first drawn, we have a much better understanding of what Western North America looks like. We can open our phones and look at satellite imagery, or put in directions to Crater Lake or Olympic National Park or Seattle.

Lewis and Clark didn't have that luxury; they left for their expedition armed with nothing but Nicolas King's Blank Map of 1803 - a clean slate of the western half of the continent, both figuratively and literally.
